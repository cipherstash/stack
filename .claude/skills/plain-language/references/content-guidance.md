# GOV.UK content guidance, for Claude's writing

ISO 24495 says what plain language has to achieve.
The UK government's content guidance turns that into specific rules, tested on millions of readers.
This file states the GOV.UK rules that apply to Claude's writing, and the ISO clause each one serves.
Where GOV.UK is stricter than ISO, follow GOV.UK.

## Start with the reader's need

Source: "Write content to meet user needs" and "Understand content design". ISO 24495-1, Principle 1.

- Before you write, name what the reader needs to do or find out.
- Write only what meets that need, and nothing more.
- Content that is no longer true has to be updated or removed (ISO 24495-1 5.4.4).

## Plain English

Source: "Use clear language". ISO 24495-1 5.3.2.

- **Plain English is for specialists too.** In the research GOV.UK cites, 80% of people preferred clear English, and the preference grew with the reader's education and expertise. Never use expertise as a reason for dense text.
- **Choose the short, common word.** Write "buy", not "purchase". Write "help", not "assist". Write "about", not "approximately".
- **Do not use buzzwords.** Replace a vague term with a description of what is being done.
- **Watch words that end in "-ion" and "-ment".** They are usually verbs turned into nouns, and they make sentences longer and harder.
- **Specialist terms are allowed, with an explanation.** Explain a term in plain words the first time you use it.
- **Use the reader's own words for things.** Call a thing what the reader calls it, not what the code or the organisation calls it.

## Words to avoid

Source: GOV.UK A to Z style guide, "Words to avoid". ISO 24495-1 5.3.2 a) and b), and 5.3.4 b).

GOV.UK lists these words because they are vague, or because they are metaphors that slow the reader down.
The list is not complete.
It shows the kind of word that confuses readers.

| Word | Write instead | Unless |
|---|---|---|
| "agenda" | "plan" | it is the list for a meeting |
| "advance" | "improve", or something specific | no exception |
| "collaborate" | "work with" | no exception |
| "combat" | "solve", "fix", or something specific | it is military |
| "commit" or "pledge" | "plan to", or "we are going to" and a specific verb | it is a git commit |
| "counter" | "prevent", or say how you solve it | no exception |
| "deliver" | "make", "create", "provide", or something specific | it is a physical delivery, or an email |
| "deploy" | "use", "build", "create" or "put into place" | it is software or military |
| "dialogue" | "spoke to" or "discussion" | no exception |
| "disincentivise" | "discourage" or "deter" | no exception |
| "drive" | "create", "cause" or "encourage" | no exception |
| "drive out" | "stop", "avoid" or "prevent" | no exception |
| "empower" | "allow" or "give permission" | no exception |
| "facilitate" | say how you help, such as "run" for a workshop | no exception |
| "focus" | "work on" or "concentrate on" | no exception |
| "foster" | "encourage" or "help" | it is about children |
| "going forward" or "moving forward" | "from now on" or "in the future" | no exception |
| "hub", "portal" or "one-stop shop" | "website" or "service" | it is part of a name |
| "impact" | "have an effect on" or "influence" | it is a collision |

| "initiate" | "start" or "begin" | no exception |
| "key" | nothing, or "important" | it unlocks something |

| "leverage" | "influence" or "use" | it is financial |
| "liaise" | "work with" or "work alongside" | no exception |
| "overarching" | nothing, or "encompassing" | no exception |

| "promote" | "recommend" or "support" | it is an advert or a job |
| "ring fencing" | "separate", or "money that will be spent on" the thing | no exception |
| "robust" | "well thought out" or "comprehensive" | it is a sturdy object |
| "slim down" | "make smaller" or "reduce the size" | no exception |
| "streamline" | "simplify", or "remove unnecessary steps" | no exception |
| "strengthening" | say what is increasing: funding, staff, attention | it is a structure |
| "tackle" | "stop", "solve" or "deal with" | it is sport or fishing |

The same guide also says:

- Write "for example", "such as" or "including", not "e.g.". Rewrite the sentence, or write "that is", instead of "i.e.". Do not use "etc.", and never end a list with it.
- Do not use a slash for "or". Write "3 or 4 times", not "3/4 times".

## Contractions

Source: "Use clear language". This is stricter than ISO 24495-1. That standard does not mention contractions.

- Do not use negative contractions such as "can't" and "don't". Many readers find them hard to read, or misread them as their opposite.
- Do not use "should've", "could've", "would've" or "they've" either.
- Positive contractions such as "you'll" are allowed.

## Must, need and can

Source: "Use clear language". ISO 24495-1 5.3.2 a).

- Write "must" for a rule that has consequences if it is broken.
- Write "need to" for a step in a process, where skipping it only stops the reader going further.
- Write "can" for a choice. Do not write "you may be able to".

## Sentence and paragraph length

Source: "Use clear language". ISO 24495-1 5.3.4 and 5.3.5.

- Use at most 5 sentences in a paragraph.
- Split up a sentence of more than 25 words.
- Length is not the test. A single paragraph full of jargon is already too long.

## Tone

Source: "Use the right tone". ISO 24495-1 5.3.7.

GOV.UK's tone is:

- specific;
- informative;
- clear and concise;
- brisk, but not terse;
- precise, and still human;
- serious, but not pompous;
- free of emotive adjectives, because they read as spin.

In practice:

- Write as if you were talking to one person, with the authority of someone who can help them.
- Use the active voice.
- Do not write "please" or "please note".
- Do not write whole phrases in capital letters. They are hard to read, and they read as shouting.
- Address the reader as "you".
- Use "they" for a person whose gender you do not know, not "he or she".
- Before you write "we", make sure the reader knows who "we" is.
- Do not use offensive language about race, ethnicity, nationality, religion, disability, mental health, gender identity, or sexual orientation.

## Structure

Source: "Create a clear structure for your content". ISO 24495-1 5.2.2.

- **Readers skim.** Most read 20 to 28% of the words on a web page, scanning across the top and then down the left side. Many are under pressure and want the answer quickly.
- **Put the most important information first,** then move to smaller and smaller details.
- **Break text up with headings,** following the rules in the next section.
- **Use lists** to make a series of items easier to read.
- **Do not use footnotes.** They were made for print. Put important information in the text, and leave the rest out.
- **Do not repeat the summary** in the first paragraph.

This rule is house style, not GOV.UK:

- **On GitHub, write each paragraph on one line.** In a pull request description, an issue, or a comment, GitHub shows each line break as a break on the page. A paragraph written one sentence per line then displays as a column of separate lines. A Markdown file in a repository is different. GitHub joins its lines into paragraphs, so one sentence per line is fine there.

## Headings

Source: "Create a clear structure for your content" and "Headings". ISO 24495-1 5.2.3 and 5.2.4.

A heading is:

- **descriptive**: it names the topic, not a generic word such as "Introduction";
- **front-loaded**: the most important words come first;
- **active**: for a task, it starts with a verb, such as "Apply for a driving licence";
- **removable**: the text still makes sense without it.

A heading is not:

- a question, because readers want answers and a question is hard to front-load;
- a technical term that the text has not explained yet.

In Markdown:

- Do not skip a heading level, such as going from a level 2 heading straight to a level 4. Screen reader users move through a page by its headings.
- Do not use a line of bold text instead of a heading. A screen reader does not recognise it as a heading.

## Links

Source: "Write effective links". ISO 24495-1 5.2.3.

- Put a link where the reader needs it, in the text. Do not collect links at the end under "Further reading" or "More information".
- Do not use more links than the reader needs, or link to the same place again and again.
- Make the link text say where the link goes. Never write "click here", "here" or "more".
- Put the important words first in the link text.
- Use different link text for different destinations, and the same link text for the same destination.
- Say if a link leaves the site, goes to a page in another language, or opens a file. For a file, give its format, and size, such as "Application form (PDF, 19.5KB)".
- For a link to information, use the page's title as the link text. For a link that starts a task, start the link text with a verb.
- Avoid links to a section of a page. When you need one, say where it goes, what to do there, and why.

## Lists

Source: "Bullet points" and "Numbered lists". ISO 24495-1 5.2.3 e).

- Use a bulleted list for a set of items, and a numbered list for steps in order.
- Introduce a list with a line that ends in a colon.
- Leave a blank line before and after a list, so it displays as a list.

## Tables

Source: "Tables". ISO 24495-3 5.3.7.

- Use a table for structured data, such as numbers or comparisons. Do not use a table where headings or a list would do.
- A table needs at least 2 columns and 3 rows, including the heading row. On a screen, about 5 columns, and 10 rows fit without scrolling.
- The first row contains the column headings. Use at most one heading row and one heading column.
- Make each heading describe its column, and do not repeat a heading.
- Put what most readers look for in the top rows and first columns.
- Do the arithmetic for the reader: add totals and differences.
- Never leave a cell empty. Write "no data" or "not applicable". Only the top-left cell can be empty.
- Do not merge or split cells, and put one item in each cell.
- In a table only, you can shorten month names, such as "Jan" and "Feb", and use a dash for a range.
- Use numerals. Round large numbers when exact ones are not needed, such as "£148.6 million".
- Right-align a column of numbers so readers can compare them.

## Titles

Source: "Write clear titles" and "Write clear summaries". Use these for a document's title, such as the first heading of a README or a report.

- Make the title unique, so a reader with several documents open can tell them apart.
- Keep it short. GOV.UK aims for 65 characters or fewer.
- Make it make sense on its own. "Regulations" says little; "Regulations for environmental waste" says what the document covers.
- Use the words readers use, not the internal or official name.
- Do not repeat yourself, and do not include the type of document, such as "guidance".
- Use a verb for a task, such as "Submit your business expenses".
- Include a date only when it tells the document apart from others in a series.

## Accessibility

Source: "Understand accessibility". ISO 24495-1 1 and 5.2.3.

Accessible writing means considering how the text is written and formatted, how it displays on phones and computers, and how it works with screen readers.
GOV.UK content has to meet the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.2, level AA.

## What the checker enforces

`scripts/check.py` reports these GOV.UK rules as "must fix":

- the words to avoid that rarely have a literal use in software writing;
- "e.g.", "i.e." and "etc.";
- negative contractions, and contractions such as "should've";
- "please note", and "he or she";
- a heading that is a question;
- a heading level that is skipped;
- link text such as "click here".

It reports these as "consider", because each one is sometimes right:

- the words to avoid that also have a literal software meaning, such as "deploy" and "promote";
- "please", and a slash used for "or";
- a generic heading, and a line of bold text used as a heading;
- a footnote;
- the same link text going to two places;
- a table that is too small, or has an empty cell;
- a column of numbers that is not right-aligned;
- percentages in a column that add up to more than 100%.
